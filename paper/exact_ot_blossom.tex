\begin{abstract}
Balanced discrete optimal transport between $n$ sources and $n$ targets of
unit mass is exactly the minimum-cost assignment problem, i.e.\ a bipartite
perfect matching (Birkhoff--von Neumann~\cite{birkhoff1946}), and can
therefore be solved \emph{exactly} by industrial matching engines
(shortest-augmenting-path assignment~\cite{jonker1987}, Edmonds' Blossom
algorithm~\cite{edmonds1965} and its Blossom~V/VI
implementations~\cite{blossomv,blossomvi}) in milliseconds to seconds. We
ask when this exact approach beats the standard approximate
alternatives---entropic Sinkhorn~\cite{sinkknopp1967,cuturi2013} and its
state-of-the-art accelerated variant Greenkhorn~\cite{awr2017,lin2022}---and
make the sparse-exact side \emph{certified} by a standard LP dual-feasibility
clip~\cite{schrijver2003}. Three contributions.
(i)~\emph{Measurement}: on dense 2-D instances, exact matching is faster
\emph{and} strictly more accurate than either approximate method throughout
the moderate-$n$ regime: $0.01\,\mathrm{s}$ at $n=500$ to
$11.5\,\mathrm{s}$ at $n=8000$; to hit a $\le 1\%$ quality target, a rough
power-law extrapolation of Greenkhorn's measured step counts puts the
approximate cost at $\sim\!10$--$\sim\!80\,\mathrm{min}$ for Greenkhorn
(factors of roughly $4{\times}10^2$--$6{\times}10^4$ over exact; plain
Sinkhorn is $20$--$650\times$ slower still). Greenkhorn, measured rather
than assumed, is within $0.93\times$--$5.0\times$ of plain Sinkhorn on the
measured cells and converges to the same entropic plan, so the quality gap
is a regularization bias shared by both. (ii)~\emph{A simple certificate}:
given a $k$NN-pool matching and its Blossom dual, a one-pass clipping of
the dual to dense feasibility gives a valid lower bound (the textbook LP
dual-feasibility argument); combined with the Sinkhorn dual potential
$\sum_i u_i+\sum_j v_j$ (which is a lower bound at \emph{every} iterate,
not just at convergence), the bound is checked on 15 instance families at
three pool sizes. (iii)~\emph{Application}: multi-robot task
allocation~\cite{gerkey2004}, where the discrete plan itself is the
deliverable: per-round myopic-exact assignment costs $0.1$--$68\,\mathrm{ms}$,
while a Sinkhorn-plus-hardening pipeline costs $0.12$--$15.9\,\mathrm{s}$
and accumulates $+6$--$27\%$ extra travel distance over 15 rounds. The
Sinkhorn family's genuine regime---large dense instances beyond the memory
wall---is acknowledged and left untouched.
All code, data, and results are open artifacts under the MIT license at
\url{https://github.com/dimkadimon/OT-Blossom}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Discrete optimal transport (OT) is a standard tool in machine learning,
statistics, imaging, and robotics \cite{peyre2019, survey2025}. The scalable
default for large instances is entropic regularization \cite{cuturi2013}:
the smooth problem is solved by the Sinkhorn iteration
\cite{sinkknopp1967}, and---for speed---by accelerated coordinate variants
such as Greenkhorn \cite{awr2017,lin2022}. Two structural facts are often
underappreciated in the approximate-OT literature.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Balanced unit-mass discrete OT \emph{is} the assignment
problem.} With $n$ sources and $n$ targets of unit mass, a transport plan is
a doubly stochastic matrix and the optimum is attained at a permutation
matrix (Birkhoff--von Neumann; Proposition~\ref{prop:int}). The problem is
therefore a bipartite minimum-weight perfect matching---a special case of
the classical assignment problem \cite{schrijver2003}, solvable exactly by
shortest-augmenting-path methods \cite{jonker1987} and, in its general
(non-bipartite) form, by Edmonds' Blossom algorithm \cite{edmonds1965} as
implemented in Blossom~V~\cite{blossomv} and VI~\cite{blossomvi}. Exact
solvers return the \emph{optimal integer plan} and its exact value. (Bipartite
matching is a strict special case of general matching, so Blossom applies
directly, but dedicated bipartite solvers such as Jonker--Volgenant or
shortest-augmenting-path are typically faster on dense bipartite inputs; we
use Blossom~VI for the sparse $k$NN-pool experiments and SciPy's
Jonker--Volgenant routine for dense solves.)
\item \textbf{Exact matching is fast.} On the 2-core/2-GB testbed of this
study, dense exact assignment takes $0.01\,\mathrm{s}$ at $n=500$ and
$11.5\,\mathrm{s}$ at $n=8000$; exploiting local structure, a $k$NN-pool
sparse solve takes $0.18\,\mathrm{s}$ at $n=2000$ with a $0.032\%$ gap to
the dense optimum (Table~5, per-side budget $k{+}1$, see Section~\ref{sec:pool});
1-D instances reach $n=10^6$ in $0.11\,\mathrm{s}$.
\end{enumerate}

The question this paper answers is empirical: \emph{when does exact
matching beat approximate OT?} We answer with (a) a measurement study in
which the approximate baselines---including Greenkhorn---are \emph{implemented
and measured, not assumed}; (b) a \emph{dual-feasibility certificate} for
the $k$NN-pool solver, so that a sparse solution comes with a rigorous,
cheaply computable bound on its distance from the dense optimum; and (c) an
\emph{application hook} in which the discrete plan is the deliverable
(multi-robot task allocation).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
\item A measured comparison of exact matching (dense Jonker--Volgenant,
  sparse Blossom~VI $k$NN pool, 1-D closed form) against vanilla Sinkhorn
  and Greenkhorn on dense, clustered, and 1-D+noise instances,
  $n\in\{500,\dots,8000\}$, multiple seeds and distributions
  (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:results}). The headline findings:
  (a)~\emph{exact is faster \emph{and} strictly more accurate than either
  approximate method throughout the moderate-$n$ regime}---dense exact runs
  in $0.01\,\mathrm{s}$ at $n=500$ to $11.5\,\mathrm{s}$ at $n=8000$,
  while reaching $\le 1\%$ quality requires $\sim\!10$--$\sim\!80$ min for
  Greenkhorn and $\sim\!200$--$\sim\!5{\times}10^4$ min for Sinkhorn (a
  rough power-law extrapolation); and (b)~Greenkhorn's measured speedup
  over plain Sinkhorn in the small-$\varepsilon$ regime needed for
  $\le 1\%$ accuracy is only $1.0$--$1.5\times$ on most cells (with one
  $5\times$ outlier, over the cells where vanilla converged; ratios on
  the two capped cells are $4.3$--$4.4\times$), while their regularization
  biases are identical.
\item A \emph{gap certificate} for the pool solver requiring no dense solve:
  a one-sided $O(n^2)$ clip of the pool matching dual to a dense-feasible
  dual (Proposition~\ref{prop:repair}, Theorem~\ref{thm:cert})---the
  textbook reduced-cost / dual-restriction operation---combined with the
  Sinkhorn-dual potential lower bound, which holds at \emph{every} iterate
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:ent}). The certificate is valid on all $45$ measured
  cells and tightens monotonically in $k$ on every family
  (Section~\ref{sec:cert}, Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item A multi-robot task-allocation experiment quantifying the ``discrete
  plan is the deliverable'' regime: myopic exact assignment is millisecond
  fast; the Sinkhorn pipeline is $10^2$--$10^3\times$ slower per round and
  accumulates extra travel distance (Section~\ref{sec:app}).
\item All code, data, and results are open artifacts under the MIT license
  (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Positioning and related work.}
We do not claim to beat near-linear-time Sinkhorn-family variants on large
dense instances ($n\gg 10^4$): that regime is genuinely theirs, and our
testbed's $2\,\mathrm{GB}$ memory wall sits near $n\approx 1.2\times 10^4$.
The contribution is to \emph{measure} the boundary in the regime where
exact is affordable, to show that Greenkhorn does not cross that boundary,
and to document how tight a standard dual-feasibility clip is as a
certificate for a sparse-$k$NN solve.

That balanced discrete OT reduces to the assignment problem is textbook
\cite{birkhoff1946,schrijver2003,villani2009,peyre2019}. $k$NN
sparsification is ubiquitous in OT practice: see the POT library
\cite{flamary2021pot}, screened Sinkhorn \cite{alaya2019screenkhorn}, and
sparse/multiscale Sinkhorn \cite{schmitzer2016,schmitzer2019stable}. The
dual-feasibility clip of Proposition~\ref{prop:repair} is the same
coordinate-wise projection used classically for reduced-cost pricing and
variable fixing in integer programming (see, e.g., the dual-reduced-cost
discussion in \cite{schrijver2003}), and an
analogous $\varepsilon$-complementary-slackness certificate for the
auction algorithm gives the classical ``within $n\varepsilon$ of optimal''
bound \cite{bertsekas1992}; we cite these precedents rather than claim
novelty for the clip itself. The log-domain Sinkhorn potentials are dual
feasible for the assignment LP at every iterate (Theorem~\ref{thm:ent}), a
fact standard in the OT literature; an entropy-corrected variant that gives
a quantitative bound on the gap is described in the lecture notes
accompanying \cite{peyre2019}.\label{pg:clip}

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{Discrete OT as assignment}
Let $C\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\ge 0}$ be a cost matrix. Write
\[
\mathcal{U}(n) \;=\; \{P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\ge 0} : P\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1},\;
P^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}\}
\]
for the set of feasible plans (each row and column sums to one; total mass
$n$). The discrete transport problem is
\[
\operatorname{OPT}(C) \;=\; \min_{P\in\mathcal{U}(n)} \; \langle C,P\rangle
\;=\; \sum_{i,j} C_{ij}P_{ij}.
\]
We use the total-mass ($n$) convention throughout, so that at an optimum
$\langle C,P\rangle$ is the sum of $n$ edge costs---the assignment cost.
(Readers comparing to references that use the unit-mass convention should
divide our reported costs by $n$.)

\begin{proposition}[OT = assignment; integer optimum]
\label{prop:int}
$\operatorname{OPT}(C)$ is attained at a permutation matrix. Equivalently,
discrete balanced unit-mass OT is the minimum-cost perfect matching in the
complete bipartite graph with edge weights $C_{ij}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the Birkhoff--von Neumann theorem \cite{birkhoff1946},
$\mathcal{U}(n)$ is the convex hull of the permutation matrices. The
objective $\langle C,\cdot\rangle$ is linear, so its minimum over the
polytope is attained at an extreme point, i.e.\ at a permutation matrix.
Equivalently, the constraint matrix of the transportation problem is
totally unimodular \cite{schrijver2003}. Under any bijection $\pi$, the
permutation matrix $P_{i,\pi(i)}=1$ is a perfect matching of the bipartite
graph with cost $\sum_i C_{i,\pi(i)}$, which proves the equivalence.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn iteration}
For $\varepsilon>0$ define the regularized problem
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weps}
W(\varepsilon) \;=\; \min_{P\in\mathcal{U}(n)}
\bigl[\,\langle C,P\rangle \;+\; \varepsilon \sum_{ij} P_{ij}\log P_{ij}\,\bigr],
\qquad (0\log 0 := 0).
\end{equation}
The minimizer is the Gibbs kernel $P^{\varepsilon}_{ij}\propto
\exp(-C_{ij}/\varepsilon)$ renormalized to have unit marginals. Write
$\operatorname{LSE}_j(a_j):=\log\sum_j e^{a_j}$ for the log-sum-exp
reduction over index $j$ (and similarly $\operatorname{LSE}_i$ over rows);
this is the standard numerically stabilized primitive (the ``LSE'' of
vectors passed in as $z$), $\operatorname{LSE}(z)=\log\sum_k e^{z_k}$.
The Sinkhorn--Knopp iteration~\cite{sinkknopp1967,cuturi2013} alternates
row and column scaling of $K=\exp(-C/\varepsilon)$; in log form, with
potentials $(u,v)$ such that
$P_{ij}=\exp\bigl((u_i+v_j-C_{ij})/\varepsilon\bigr)$, each full row pass
sets
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sinkupdate}
u_i \;\leftarrow\; -\varepsilon\,\operatorname{LSE}_j\Bigl(\frac{v_j-C_{ij}}{\varepsilon}\Bigr)
\;=\; u_i-\varepsilon\log\!\sum_j P_{ij},
\end{equation}
and symmetrically for $v_j$.
We run the log-domain variant, stopping at maximum marginal deviation
$\le 10^{-9}$ or on a cost plateau.

\textbf{Two different quantities.} Equation~\eqref{eq:weps} matches the
standard entropic-OT form used in \cite{cuturi2013,peyre2019}: with the
Shannon entropy $H(P)=-\sum_{ij} P_{ij}\log P_{ij}$ (taking $0\log 0=0$),
the regularizer $+\varepsilon\sum P_{ij}\log P_{ij}$ is $-\varepsilon H(P)$,
so $W(\varepsilon)$ is exactly the Cuturi/Peyr\'e--Cuturi Sinkhorn value.
Two different numbers are routinely compared to OPT and are easily confused:
(i) the \emph{transport cost} of the entropic plan, $\langle C,P^\varepsilon\rangle$,
which is always $\ge\mathrm{OPT}$ because $P^\varepsilon$ is a feasible
transport plan---this is the ``gap'' column in Tables~\ref{tab:approx}
and~\ref{tab:ghead}; and (ii) the \emph{regularized value}
$W(\varepsilon)=\sum_i u_i+\sum_j v_j$, which is always $\le\mathrm{OPT}$
by weak duality (Theorem~\ref{thm:ent}). Algorithm~\ref{alg:sink} returns
the dual value $\sum_i u_i+\sum_j v_j$, which coincides with
$\langle C,P\rangle+\varepsilon\sum P_{ij}\log P_{ij}$ at convergence.

Greenkhorn \cite{awr2017} replaces the cyclic pass by a greedy
single-coordinate update: each step picks the most-violated row or column
(measured by the KL-type residual $\rho(a,b)=b-a+a\log(a/b)$) and applies
the exact closed-form update that restores that one marginal, at $O(n)$
cost per step with incremental marginal bookkeeping
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:green}). Early Greenkhorn analyses
\cite{awr2017} gave an $\tilde O(n^2/\varepsilon^3)$ total-work bound in
terms of additive accuracy; the tighter $\tilde O(n^2/\varepsilon^2)$
bound is from later work \cite{lin2022}.

\subsection{Exact solvers used}
\paragraph{Dense.} We use SciPy's optimized Jonker--Volgenant-style
shortest-augmenting-path implementation \texttt{linear\_sum\_assignment}
\cite{jonker1987} (a modified JV algorithm in the spirit of Crouse), with
empirical roughly-cubic scaling and $O(n^2)$ memory on dense inputs; see
Section~\ref{sec:results}.
\paragraph{Sparse pool.} For a pool $E_k\subseteq [n]\times[n]$ (the $k$
nearest targets of each source, symmetrically), solve the assignment
restricted to $E_k$ as a minimum-weight perfect matching with our
edge-list driver for Blossom~VI \cite{blossomvi} (Blossom~V \cite{blossomv}
gives identical matching costs; see Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Costs are
integer (milliunits) as required by the driver. We note that the pool
problem is bipartite, so any bipartite sparse LAP solver (shortest
augmenting path, cost-scaling, auction with $\varepsilon$-scaling) could be
substituted; the Blossom~VI driver was convenient because the same code
base was used for prior non-bipartite matching experiments.
\paragraph{1-D.} When sources and targets lie on a line (or on two parallel
near-lines, our ``1-D+noise'' family), the optimal map is the sorted
identity (Proposition~\ref{prop:1d}), solvable in $O(n\log n)$ sort time
with no matrix formed.

\subsection{One-dimensional closed form}
When both point sets are supported on the line, OT admits a closed-form
solution via sorting. This is the classical quantile (monotone) map:

\begin{proposition}[1-D optimality of the quantile map]
\label{prop:1d}
Let $x_{(1)}\le\cdots\le x_{(n)}$, $y_{(1)}\le\cdots\le y_{(n)}$. For the
quadratic cost, and more generally for any convex function $h$ applied to
the pairwise difference (cost $h(x_i-y_j)$; this includes $|x_i-y_j|$ used
in the 1-D+noise experiments), the identity map $x_{(i)}\mapsto y_{(i)}$
is \emph{an} optimal bijection.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
It suffices to show no crossing is present in an optimal map. Suppose
$\pi$ has a crossing: $i<i'$ with $\pi(i)>\pi(i')$. With
$a=x_{(i)}\le b=x_{(i')}$ and $c=y_{\pi(i')}\le d=y_{\pi(i)}$, the
convexity of $h$ gives the Monge inequality
\[
h(a-c)+h(b-d) \;\le\; h(a-d)+h(b-c),
\]
so uncrossing the pair does not increase the cost. Repeated uncrossing
reduces the number of inversions and terminates at a non-crossing
(monotone) bijection, which is the identity up to ties
\cite[Ch.~2]{villani2009}.
\end{proof}

\section{Approximate baselines: implementation and validation}
\label{sec:approx}

Both approximate methods are implemented in the log domain (NumPy,
single-threaded) and stopped at a marginal tolerance of $10^{-9}$ on the
dense grid, or on a cost plateau at the coarser tolerances
$10^{-6}$--$10^{-3}$ typical of Greenkhorn at small $f$. The regularization
is parameterized as a fraction of the mean entry of the cost matrix,
$f=\varepsilon/\overline{C}$ where $\overline{C}=n^{-2}\sum_{ij}C_{ij}$, on
the grid $f\in\{0.05,0.02,0.01\}$ (plus $0.005$ at $n=500$).

Two validation facts structure the comparison. \emph{First}, Greenkhorn and
vanilla Sinkhorn converge to the \emph{identical} entropic plan up to the
stated stopping tolerance at every measured $(n,f)$ (cost agreement
$\le 0.04\%$, Table~\ref{tab:ghead}), so the comparison between them is
purely a wall-time comparison; the gap to exact is the \emph{regularization
bias}, common to both. \emph{Second}, a Sinkhorn plan is fractional; where
an integer plan is required it must be \emph{hardened} (e.g.\ the
row-argmax permutation, our \texttt{greedy\_hard}). Hardening a converged
$n=500$, $f=0.01$ plan (gap $5.4\%$) costs $+10.9\%$ over the exact
optimum---which the exact solver returns, optimally, in $0.01\,\mathrm{s}$.

We emphasize that these baselines are our own single-threaded NumPy
implementations and are not tuned the way a library implementation (e.g.\
POT \cite{flamary2021pot}) would be; our timing claims are therefore
statements about these specific implementations on our testbed, not about
fundamental limits of Sinkhorn/Greenkhorn.

\section{Duality certificates for the pool solver}
\label{sec:cert}

This section gives a rigorous certificate for the pool solver that
requires \emph{no} dense solve. It is a standard LP weak-duality argument
applied to a clipped dual; what is new is the empirical documentation of
how tight the bound is across families and~$k$.

\paragraph{Setup.} The pool solver returns a perfect matching $M$ inside
$E_k$, with cost $\mathrm{UB}=\sum_{(i,j)\in M} C_{ij}$. Since $M$ is a
feasible dense solution, $\mathrm{OPT}\le \mathrm{UB}$; $\mathrm{UB}$ is a
trivial upper bound. What a certificate must supply is a lower bound
$\mathrm{LB}\le\mathrm{OPT}$ that is \emph{cheap and rigorous}. Blossom~VI
returns, together with the pool matching, the dual of the pool problem:
node potentials $(\alpha_i,\beta_j)$ with
$\alpha_i+\beta_j\le C_{ij}$ on pool edges and
$\sum_i\alpha_i+\sum_j\beta_j=\mathrm{UB}$ (complementary slackness; the
blossom duals vanish for bipartite instances, see Section~\ref{sec:prelim}).
The dual is \emph{not} feasible for the dense problem (it may violate
$\alpha_i+\beta_j\le C_{ij}$ off the pool), so it is not by itself a lower
bound on $\mathrm{OPT}$. The repair below fixes exactly that.

\begin{proposition}[One-sided dual clip]
\label{prop:repair}
For any vectors $(\alpha,\beta)$ (no assumption required), define
\[
\hat\beta_j \;=\; \min\Bigl(\beta_j,\; \min_i \bigl[C_{ij}-\alpha_i\bigr]\Bigr),
\qquad
\hat\alpha_i \;=\; \min\Bigl(\alpha_i,\; \min_j \bigl[C_{ij}-\beta_j\bigr]\Bigr).
\]
Then both $(\alpha,\hat\beta)$ and $(\hat\alpha,\beta)$ are dual-feasible
for the \emph{dense} assignment problem:
$\alpha_i+\hat\beta_j\le C_{ij}$ and $\hat\alpha_i+\beta_j\le C_{ij}$ for
\emph{all} $(i,j)\in[n]^2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix any pair $(i,j)$. By definition,
$\hat\beta_j\le C_{ij}-\alpha_i$, since $i$ is one of the indices over
which the inner minimum is taken. Hence
$\alpha_i+\hat\beta_j\le C_{ij}$ for all $(i,j)$. The second statement is
symmetric. $\square$
\end{proof}

\noindent The clip needs no hypothesis on $(\alpha,\beta)$: it is simply a
coordinate-wise projection of an arbitrary pair of vectors onto the dual
feasible set of the assignment LP, i.e.\ the classical reduced-cost
projection used in LP pricing and variable fixing. When $(\alpha,\beta)$
comes from a pool optimum the clipped vectors are typically close to the
true dual, which is what makes the bound empirically tight.

\begin{theorem}[Pool gap certificate]
\label{thm:cert}
Let $\mathrm{UB}$ be the pool matching cost and set
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lb1}
\mathrm{LB}_1 \;=\; \max\Bigl(
\textstyle\sum_i\alpha_i+\sum_j\hat\beta_j,\;\;
\sum_i\hat\alpha_i+\sum_j\beta_j
\Bigr).
\end{equation}
Then $\mathrm{LB}_1\le \mathrm{OPT}(C)\le \mathrm{UB}$, and the pool
solution is certified to be within $\mathrm{UB}-\mathrm{LB}_1$ in absolute
terms (relative width $(\mathrm{UB}-\mathrm{LB}_1)/\mathrm{UB}$, and
$(\mathrm{UB}-\mathrm{LB}_1)/\mathrm{LB}_1$ when $\mathrm{LB}_1>0$) of the
dense optimum---without solving the dense problem.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:repair}, both repaired pairs are dense dual
feasible; by weak duality for the assignment LP \cite{schrijver2003}, each
of their objectives is a lower bound on $\mathrm{OPT}(C)$, hence so is
their maximum. The upper bound $\mathrm{OPT}\le\mathrm{UB}$ holds because
the pool matching is a feasible dense solution. The gap statements are
immediate algebra from $\mathrm{LB}_1\le\mathrm{OPT}\le\mathrm{UB}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:gap}
The certified width is never smaller than the true gap:
$(\mathrm{UB}-\mathrm{LB}_1)/\mathrm{UB}\;\ge\;(\mathrm{UB}-\mathrm{OPT})/\mathrm{UB}$.
The certificate can be loose; it is never wrong.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Ordering of the repair]
\label{rem:order}
Let $A=(\alpha,\hat\beta)$ and $B=(\hat\alpha,\beta)$ be the two one-sided
clips of Proposition~\ref{prop:repair}. A natural alternative is a
sequential two-pass variant $S=(\hat\alpha,\tilde\beta)$: first clip $\alpha$
against $\beta$ to get $\hat\alpha$, then clip $\beta$ against
$\hat\alpha$. Because the second pass only lowers entries of $\beta$
($\tilde\beta_j\le\beta_j$), we have $\sum_i\hat\alpha_i+\sum_j\tilde\beta_j
\le \sum_i\hat\alpha_i+\sum_j\beta_j$, so $S$ is \emph{always dominated}
by $B$; it is incomparable with $A$, as explicit $n=2$ witnesses
show. With $C=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}7&12\\13&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$,
$\alpha=(-10,9)$, $\beta=(14,7)$: one-sided repairs give
$\hat\beta=(4,-7)$, $\hat\alpha=(-10,-5)$ with objectives $A=-4$, $B=6$,
and a second clip leaves $\tilde\beta=(14,7)$ so $S=6>A$.
With $C=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}17&6\\4&10\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$,
$\alpha=(16,15)$, $\beta=(15,-5)$:
$\hat\beta=(-11,-10)$, $\hat\alpha=(2,-11)$ give $A=10$, $B=1$,
and $S=1<A$. (Random trials on larger instances find $S>A$ and $S<A$
about equally often, $\approx 20\%$ each.) Since
$\max(A,B)\ge B\ge S$, the max-of-two rule \eqref{eq:lb1} is never worse
than the sequential variant while costing the same $O(n^2)$ work, and we
use it throughout.
Further alternating beyond two passes would only decrease the dual
objective (each clip is a coordinate-wise min, which cannot raise any
entry), so additional cheap clips cannot improve the bound. Driving the
certified width to zero requires adding violated edges (pricing / column
generation), which we leave to future work.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Sinkhorn potentials are a dual lower bound]
\label{thm:ent}
Let $(u,v)$ be \emph{any} potentials satisfying $u_i+v_j\le C_{ij}$ for all
$i,j$. Then $\sum_i u_i+\sum_j v_j\le \operatorname{OPT}(C)$, with equality
if and only if $(u,v)$ is an optimal dual for the assignment LP.
In particular, log-domain Sinkhorn iterates produce such a dual-feasible
pair $(u,v)$ at \emph{every} step, so $\sum_i u_i+\sum_j v_j$ is a rigorous
lower bound on $\operatorname{OPT}$ at any stopping point, with no
convergence requirement and no feasibility restoration. At convergence of
the smooth problem the dual value coincides with $W(\varepsilon)$ in
\eqref{eq:weps}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The first sentence is weak duality for the assignment LP
\cite{schrijver2003}. For the automatic dual feasibility of Sinkhorn
iterates: Algorithm~\ref{alg:sink} updates the columns last (line 7), so
immediately after a column pass every \emph{column} of $P$ sums to $1$;
since $P\ge 0$, every entry satisfies $P_{ij}\le 1$, hence
$u_i+v_j-C_{ij}=\varepsilon\log P_{ij}\le 0$. The same holds immediately
after any row pass, using row sums of $1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[On the entropic dual]
\label{rem:num}
The Sinkhorn dual $\sum_i u_i+\sum_j v_j$ is valid at every iterate
(Theorem~\ref{thm:ent}), which removes any need for a $\delta$-feasibility
restoration. At usable $\varepsilon$ the bound is typically loose because
the negative entropy term is large: at $n=2000$, $\varepsilon\approx 0.05\cdot 52\approx 2.6$
gives an entropy contribution on the order of $-\varepsilon n\log n\approx -4\times 10^4$,
far below OPT. Better entropy-corrected variants
$\mathrm{LB}_{\varepsilon}=D_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon H(a)-\varepsilon H(b)$
that quantitatively bound $0\le\mathrm{OPT}-\mathrm{LB}_{\varepsilon}\le\delta$
are described in the lecture notes accompanying \cite{peyre2019}
(see also the discussion on p.~\pageref{pg:clip} above) and would
strengthen the bound at small extra cost; we leave them to future work.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Combined certificate.} We report
$\mathrm{LB}=\max(\mathrm{LB}_1,\, \sum_i u_i+\sum_j v_j)$ with
$\varepsilon=0.05\,\overline{C}$ whenever the Sinkhorn run converges in
time. In every measured instance the dual-repair bound $\mathrm{LB}_1$
dominates the Sinkhorn dual at $k\ge 16$; the Sinkhorn dual is reported
as a labeled cross-check.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Testbed.} 2-core / 2-GB sandbox; single-threaded Python 3.13
with NumPy and SciPy's Jonker--Volgenant-style assignment routine for the
dense exact, Sinkhorn, and Greenkhorn codes; a C++20 build of Blossom~VI
\cite{blossomvi} (edge-list driver, integer milliunit costs) for the pool
solves; and Blossom~V \cite{blossomv}, used only to cross-validate
matching costs (identical on every instance tested). All costs are rounded
to milliunits for the integer solvers, and all gaps are computed on this
same rounding. \textbf{Timings are single runs} (single-threaded wall time,
same machine, same process order) and are reported for reproducibility on
this hardware; they are not averaged over multiple runs and should be
read as order-of-magnitude rather than as benchmark-grade timings.

\paragraph{Instance families} (2-D Euclidean distances; details in
Appendix~\ref{app:detail}).
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{uniform}: i.i.d.\ points in $[0,100)^2$.
\item \textbf{clustered}: 8 cluster centers in $[0,100)^2$; each side's
  points are assigned round-robin to a center with Gaussian noise of
  standard deviation $\sigma=8$; the two sides use the same assignment
  pattern, with target centers jittered by isotropic Gaussian noise of
  standard deviation $3$ (i.e.\ $\mathcal{N}(0,3^2 I)$).
\item \textbf{1-D+noise}: one-dimensional base positions on two parallel
  noisy lines (along-line noise $\sigma=2$; cross-line noise $\sigma=2$);
  see Appendix~\ref{app:detail}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Seeds.} Each family is parameterized by a NumPy
\texttt{default\_rng(seed)} (the seed fixes source positions; target
positions for the 1-D+noise family use \texttt{default\_rng(seed+1000)},
see Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). We report the headline dense scaling and
quality figures on a single canonical instance (\textbf{seed 7}, uniform
distribution), which we chose before running any experiments as the
default seed and kept throughout so that tables and figures line up on
one reproducible instance; robustness across seeds 7--10 is reported
separately. The dense grid spans $n\in\{500,1000,2000,4000,8000\}$ on
that canonical instance ($n=12000$ runs out of memory after interpreter
and NumPy temporaries: a $12000^2$ double matrix is $1.15\,\mathrm{GB}$,
which leaves too little headroom on a 2-GB sandbox). The robustness
sweep covers $n=1000$, seeds 7--10, all three families (12
configurations); the certificate study adds $n=2000$, seed 7, all three
families (15 configurations total). The multi-robot application uses
$n\in\{50,200,800\}$, seeds 7--8, 15 rounds.

\paragraph{Reading.}
(i)~\emph{Validity}: the certificate is a theorem; the 45/45 check is a
test of the implementation, not of the math. (ii)~\emph{Tightness}: where
the pool is tight (uniform instances), the width is tight too---$\le 0.70\%$
at $k=32$ against a true gap $\le 0.13\%$, and $\le 0.02\%$ at $k=64$.
On block-structured (clustered) and near-Monge (1-D+noise) families the
repair is conservative, because the pool dual is large where it must be
clipped against cheap off-pool edges (up to $21.88\%$ at $k=32$, true gap
$1.73\%$). The bound remains \emph{valid}, and the width tightens
monotonically in $k$ on every configuration, so widening the pool is a
certified escape hatch. A standard column-generation / pricing loop
(price off-pool edges with the clipped dual, add the most-violated ones,
re-solve) typically drives the certified width to zero for a fraction of
the dense-solve cost; this is the natural next step and is left to future
work. (iii)~\emph{Cost}: the clip itself is an $O(n^2)$ pass over the
cost matrix and therefore has the same memory footprint as dense
assignment; on our $n=2000$ cells it adds $\le 0.73\,\mathrm{s}$ to the
pool solve.

\subsection{Robustness across seeds and distributions}
On the 12-configuration $n=1000$ sweep (seeds 7--10 $\times$ families):
exact is fastest everywhere ($0.07$--$0.10\,\mathrm{s}$, consistent with
the $0.06\,\mathrm{s}$ at seed-7 uniform given seed/family variation) and
optimal; the $k=32$ pool solver is feasible in all cells with gaps
$+0.010\%$ to $+2.599\%$ in $<0.15\,\mathrm{s}$; Greenkhorn at $f=0.01$
sits at $+6.3\%$ to $+14.7\%$ (agreeing with vanilla to $\le 0.05\%$ in
the converged cells); vanilla needs $\ge 400\,\mathrm{s}$ without
converging on the symmetric clustered configurations, where Greenkhorn's
plateau matches it in $50$--$98\,\mathrm{s}$.

\subsection{1-D structure}
The quantile map solves the 1-D family in $O(n\log n)$ sort time with no
matrix: $n=10^6$ in $0.11\,\mathrm{s}$, where the dense cost matrix alone
would be $8\,\mathrm{TB}$.

\section{Application: multi-robot task allocation}
\label{sec:app}

The clearest regime for the discrete plan as deliverable is multi-robot
task allocation \cite{gerkey2004}: in warehouse picking, drone teaming, or
AMR dispatch, each of $n$ robots must be assigned \emph{exactly one} of
$n$ tasks---a fractional transport plan is physically meaningless (one
cannot dispatch half a robot). The standard practice in MRTA is precisely
the Hungarian/auction family of exact solvers
\cite{bertsekas1988, jonker1987, gerkey2004}; we include this experiment
as a sanity check on a regime where integer plans are required, not as a
novel robotics result.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Open field $[0,100)^2$; 15 rounds; each round, $n$
fresh tasks appear, robots are (re)assigned with cost
$C_{ij}=\lVert r_i-t_j\rVert_2$ from the robots' current positions
$r_i\in\mathbb{R}^2$ to the new task locations $t_j\in\mathbb{R}^2$, and
robots then move to their assigned tasks (the post-move positions become
the next round's $r_i$). All three policies share the \emph{same} start
positions and the \emph{same} task stream per round, so only the
per-round assignment differs. Policies: \textbf{exact} (myopic dense
bipartite LAP per round via SciPy's Jonker--Volgenant routine; we use the
dense bipartite solver rather than Blossom~VI because the cost matrix is
fully dense at the $n\le 800$ scale of this experiment, where a dedicated
bipartite shortest-augmenting-path solver is the faster and simpler
choice); \textbf{greedy} (cheapest-first serial assignment);
\textbf{Sinkhorn+hardening} (our $f=0.05$, marginal tolerance $10^{-7}$
pipeline, then greedy hardening to an integer plan).
$n\in\{50,200,800\}$, seeds 7--8; Table~\ref{tab:robot} reports the
episode totals and Figure~\ref{fig:robot} reports both cumulative travel
and per-round assignment wall time.

\textbf{Caveat on optimality.} Because each round's assignment determines
the next round's robot positions, per-round myopic optimality does
\emph{not} minimize the 15-round total distance (the true episode optimum
requires dynamic programming / rollout / lookahead). The ``gap'' columns
in Table~\ref{tab:robot} are therefore gaps relative to the myopic-exact
policy, not to the global episode optimum. Even so, the comparison is
informative: the Sinkhorn pipeline is both slower per round and yields
larger cumulative travel than myopic-exact, because the regularization
bias pushes assignments away from the nearest-task plan in a way that
compounds across rounds.

\paragraph{Findings.}
(i)~\emph{The quality tax accumulates}: per-round gaps compound through
robot positions; over 15 rounds greedy ends $+17$--$+37\%$ and
Sinkhorn+hardening $+6$--$+27\%$ above the myopic-exact policy, and the
gap grows with $n$. (ii)~\emph{Approximate is not even faster here}: the
assignment step takes myopic-exact $0.1$--$68\,\mathrm{ms}$/round vs
$0.12$--$15.9\,\mathrm{s}$ for the Sinkhorn pipeline ($\approx 239\times$
at $n=800$). (iii)~\emph{The hook}: where a discrete integer plan is the
deliverable, a Sinkhorn-plus-hardening pipeline is a tax on \emph{both}
time and quality at $n\le 10^3$; an exact bipartite solver is the default
choice, all the way up to the memory wall.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

\paragraph{Empirical decision rule.}
Integer plan needed $\to$ exact; 1-D/Monge $\to$ closed form; local/$k$NN
structure $\to$ sparse pool exact, \emph{with its certificate}
(Section~\ref{sec:cert}); dense, no structure, fractional plan
acceptable $\to$ exact up to the memory wall ($n\approx 1.2\times 10^4$
on this hardware), beyond which well-tuned Sinkhorn-family
implementations win on time and memory.

\paragraph{What the certificate is, and what it is not.}
The clip of Proposition~\ref{prop:repair} is a textbook dual projection,
not a new theorem; its value is engineering: the solver can report ``my
solution is within $w\%$ of optimal'' with $w$ computed from its own
dual in milliseconds, using an operation that costs one $O(n^2)$ pass.
Where a $\le 1\%$ guarantee is a requirement (dispatching, pricing,
auditing), this replaces a dense re-solve or a conservative safety margin.
The certificate is loose on clustered families (up to $\sim 22\%$ at
$k=32$) and is currently computed as a one-shot clip; we expect that a
standard pricing/column-generation loop (add off-pool edges with negative
reduced cost, re-solve) would drive the certified width to zero for a
fraction of the dense cost, which we leave to future work.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
(i) Two-core/2-GB testbed and single-run timings: wall times are
hardware-specific and should not be read as benchmarks; only the
\emph{qualitative} comparisons (exact is milliseconds to seconds,
Sinkhorn is 100--1000$\times$ slower at small $n$) are intended to
generalize. (ii) The $f^*$ extrapolation (Table~\ref{tab:target}) is a
rough power-law projection, as documented, and carries $\sim$order-of-magnitude
model uncertainty. (iii) The certificate is conservative on block-structured
instances (Table~\ref{tab:cert}); a structure-aware repair or a
column-generation loop is left to future work. (iv) The application is
synthetic (no obstacles, kinematics, or traffic), and myopic per-round
assignment is not globally optimal over an episode; the point is
structural, not a robotics benchmark. (v) We do not compare against
auction algorithms with $\varepsilon$-scaling, dedicated bipartite sparse
LAP solvers, stabilized/$\varepsilon$-annealed Sinkhorn, or GPU
implementations; those are important baselines for a full benchmark paper.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

Several natural extensions follow directly from this study.

\emph{Tightening the certificate.} The one-shot dual clip
(Proposition~\ref{prop:repair}) is valid but loose on clustered families
(up to $\sim 22\%$ at $k=32$). A standard pricing / column-generation
loop---use the clipped dual to identify off-pool edges with negative
reduced cost, add them to the pool, re-solve---should drive the certified
width to zero at a small fraction of the dense-solve cost, essentially
turning the pool solver into a sparse dual active-set method. A cheaper
alternative worth exploring is to apply the clip to \emph{both} sides
alternately until convergence (Remark~\ref{rem:order} noted that two
passes is the max for the sequential variant, but a symmetric alternating
scheme with a tight line search may improve the bound further).

\emph{Stronger baselines.} Adding auction with $\varepsilon$-scaling,
shortest-augmenting-path sparse bipartite LAP (e.g.\ a cost-scaling
implementation), stabilized / $\varepsilon$-annealed Sinkhorn, and GPU
POT~\cite{flamary2021pot} would make the benchmark more complete and is
the main missing axis of comparison.

\emph{Entropy-corrected lower bounds.} The Sinkhorn dual used here is
$\sum u_i+\sum_j v_j = W(\varepsilon)$, which is a lower bound at every
iterate but is loose (by $\sim\varepsilon n\log n$) because it includes
the negative entropy. The entropy-corrected variant
$\mathrm{LB}_\varepsilon=D_\varepsilon-\varepsilon H(a)-\varepsilon H(b)$
described in the lecture notes accompanying~\cite{peyre2019} can close
most of that gap and should dominate the dual-repair bound at small $k$;
combining it with the pool clip is algorithmically straightforward.

\emph{Beyond myopic assignment in the application.} The multi-robot
experiment uses per-round myopic assignment. A natural next step is to
integrate the exact solver into a non-myopic planner (lookahead,
rollout, or auction-based coordination with task deadlines), where the
cheap per-round exact call becomes a subroutine.

\emph{Scaling beyond the memory wall.} Above $n\approx 10^4$ the dense
cost matrix no longer fits in memory, and genuine large-$n$ OT requires
either Sinkhorn-family methods or a hierarchical / multi-scale sparse
scheme; quantifying where a hierarchical Blossom-based solver breaks
even against a well-tuned GPU Sinkhorn is the practical question for
follow-on work.

\section{Conclusion}
Balanced discrete OT is the assignment problem, and the assignment problem
is a bipartite matching problem, so industrial matching engines give exact
optimal transport with an integer plan and an exact value. Measured
against hand-written NumPy Sinkhorn and Greenkhorn baselines on the
moderate-$n$ regime, exact matching is faster \emph{and} more accurate;
Greenkhorn's speedup over plain Sinkhorn in the small-$\varepsilon$ regime
needed for $\le 1\%$ quality is only $\sim$1--$1.5\times$ on most cells,
and it inherits the full regularization bias. We documented how tight a
textbook one-pass dual clip is as a certificate for a $k$NN-pool solve
across 15 instance families, and showed in a multi-robot sanity check that
where an integer plan is the deliverable, approximate OT is a tax on both
time and quality. The Sinkhorn family's large-$n$ dense regime stands
untouched, and the boundary between exact and approximate is now measured.

\section{Additional proofs and details}
\label{app:proofs}

\subsection{Worked example of the certificate}
\label{app:example}
Consider
\[
C=\begin{pmatrix}
57&14&33&40\\
36&29&59&11\\
49&40&58&26\\
38&33&53&42
\end{pmatrix}
\]
with the $2$-NN pool (the union of each row's and each column's two
cheapest edges; 10 of the 16 edges). The pool solver returns the matching
of cost $\mathrm{UB}=122$ and the pool dual
$\alpha=(7,18,33,20)$, $\beta=(18,7,26,-7)$, with
$\sum\alpha+\sum\beta=122=\mathrm{UB}$ and
$\alpha_i+\beta_j\le C_{ij}$ on every pool edge.

\emph{The raw dual is not dense-feasible.} Among the six off-pool edges
$\{(1,1),(1,4),(2,3),(3,1),(3,3),(4,4)\}$, two are violated:
$\alpha_3+\beta_1=33+18=51>49=C_{31}$ and
$\alpha_3+\beta_3=33+26=59>58=C_{33}$. (The dual objective of $122$ even
exceeds the dense optimum---infeasible duals can.) The dense optimum is
$\mathrm{OPT}=121$ (true pool gap $0.83\%$), attained by the matching
$(1,2),(2,4),(3,3),(4,1)$ of costs $14+11+58+38=121$.

\emph{Repair.} Option~B (clip the $\alpha$ side against the original
$\beta$): $\hat\alpha_3=\min\bigl(33,\min_j(C_{3j}-\beta_j)\bigr)
=\min(33,\,49-18,\,40-7,\,58-26,\,26+7)=31$; the other $\alpha_i$ need no
clipping. Objective: $(7+18+31+20)+(18+7+26-7)=120$. Option~A (clip
the $\beta$ side against the original $\alpha$) gives
$\hat\beta=(16,7,25,-7)$ with objective $119$. Taking the max
(Theorem~\ref{thm:cert}),
\[
\mathrm{LB}=120\;\le\;\mathrm{OPT}=121\;\le\;\mathrm{UB}=122,
\]
so the pool solution is certified within $\frac{122-120}{122}=1.64\%$
(width) of the dense optimum. In code the repair is two $O(n^2)$ min-passes.

\subsection{Monotonicity of the upper bound in $k$}
Since $E_k\subseteq E_{k'}$ for $k\le k'$, every $k$-pool matching is a
valid $k'$-pool candidate, hence
$\mathrm{UB}_{k'}\le \mathrm{UB}_k$; the pool gap is non-increasing in $k$
by definition. The certified width is monotone in $k$ on all 15 measured
configurations (an empirical fact about the \emph{repair}, not a theorem:
the pool dual changes with $k$).

\section{Experimental details}
\label{app:detail}

\subsection{Instance generators}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{uniform}: $\mathrm{rng}=\texttt{default\_rng(seed)}$; both
  sides $=100\cdot \mathrm{rng.random}((n,2))$.
\item \textbf{clustered}: centers $c_a=100\cdot\mathrm{rng.random}((8,2))$;
  each side assigns point $i$ to center $a=i\bmod 8$ with
  $x_i=c_{a}+\mathcal N(0,8^2 I)$; the target centers are
  $c'_a=c_a+\mathcal N(0,3^2 I)$ with the same assignment pattern (target
  jitter $\sigma=3$).
\item \textbf{1-D+noise}: $t_i\sim U[0,100]$;
  $x_i=(t_i+\mathcal N(0,2^2),\,\mathcal N(0,2^2))$; independently
  $s_j\sim U[0,100]$ from $\texttt{default\_rng(seed+1000)}$;
  $y_j=(s_j+\mathcal N(0,2^2),\,\mathcal N(0,2^2))$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Algorithms}
\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Log-domain Sinkhorn (vanilla), after~\cite{sinkknopp1967,cuturi2013}}\label{alg:sink}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require cost $C\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$, $\varepsilon>0$, tolerance $\tau$
\State $u\leftarrow 0$, $v\leftarrow 0$
\Repeat
  \For{$i=1,\dots,n$}
    \State $u_i\;\leftarrow\; -\varepsilon\,\mathrm{LSE}_j\bigl((v_j-C_{ij})/\varepsilon\bigr)$
  \EndFor
  \For{$j=1,\dots,n$}
    \State $v_j\;\leftarrow\; -\varepsilon\,\mathrm{LSE}_i\bigl((u_i-C_{ij})/\varepsilon\bigr)$
  \EndFor
  \State $P_{ij}\leftarrow \exp((u_i+v_j-C_{ij})/\varepsilon)$
\Until $\max_i|(P\mathbf 1)_i-1|+\max_j|(P^{\mathsf T}\mathbf 1)_j-1|\le\tau$ \emph{or cost plateau}
\State \Return $P$, value $\sum_i u_i+\sum_j v_j$ ($=\langle C,P\rangle+\varepsilon\sum P_{ij}\log P_{ij}$ at convergence)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Greenkhorn (after \cite[Alg.~4]{awr2017})}\label{alg:green}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $K=\exp(-C/\varepsilon)$ (unnormalized), potentials $x,y=0$,
residual metric $\rho(a,b)=b-a+a\log(a/b)$
\State maintain row/column marginals of $A=D(e^x)K D(e^y)$ incrementally
\Repeat
  \State $I\leftarrow\arg\max_i \rho(1,(A\mathbf 1)_i)$;
  $J\leftarrow\arg\max_j \rho(1,(\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}A)_j)$
  \If{$\rho_I>\rho_J$}
    \State $x_I\leftarrow x_I+\log(1/(A\mathbf 1)_I)$
  \Else
    \State $y_J\leftarrow y_J+\log(1/(\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}A)_J)$
    \Comment{break ties toward rows when $\rho_I=\rho_J$}
  \EndIf
  \State update $A$ and the marginals of the touched line ($O(n)$)
\Until marginal tolerance or cost plateau
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Hardening} (\texttt{greedy\_hard}): for $i=1,\dots,n$, set
$\pi(i)=\arg\max_j P_{ij}$ among unused columns, removing row $i$ and
column $\pi(i)$ from $P$; the cost is $\sum_i C_{i,\pi(i)}$ (a feasible
integer plan, hence $\ge \mathrm{OPT}$).

\textbf{Pool construction} (\texttt{knn\_pool}): for each source $i$, keep
the $k$ targets with smallest $C_{ij}$ (and symmetrically for each target);
the pool is the union. If the pool has no perfect matching (Hall's
condition fails, e.g.\ $k=1$), the solver reports infeasible. The
repository version uses $k$ per side (corrected); the cached results were
generated by a version that used $k+1$ per side (see Table~\ref{tab:pool}).

\textbf{Certificate} (\texttt{pool\_certified}):
UB/perm/dual from \texttt{blossom\_pool\_duals};
$\mathrm{LB}_1$ from Proposition~\ref{prop:repair} (max of the two
one-sided repairs); $\mathrm{LB}_2=\sum_i u_i+\sum_j v_j$ from a converged
Sinkhorn run at $\varepsilon=0.05\,\overline C$ per configuration (shared
across $k$); $\mathrm{LB}=\max(\mathrm{LB}_1,\mathrm{LB}_2)$.

\subsection{Parameters}
Marginal tolerance $10^{-9}$ on the dense grid (application hook: $10^{-7}$,
since an integer plan is produced regardless); time caps $400\,$s (dense
grid), $240\,\mathrm{s}$ (certificate-study Sinkhorn runs); Blossom~VI pool
timeout $120\,\mathrm{s}$ (never hit); costs rounded to milliunits for the
integer solvers; all timings single-threaded wall time, same machine, same
process order, single run per cell.

and figures regenerate without it, and the pool upper-bound costs can
be verified independently with any bipartite LAP solver on the same
$k$NN subgraph.
